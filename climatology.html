Insolation
  • Earth = oblate ellipsoid; mean sun distance ≈ 149.6 million km. Only a small fraction of solar output reaches earth = insolation.
  • Total received is nearly constant (minor change at perihelion/aphelion); its distribution varies.
  • Perihelion ~3 Jan (147 million km); Aphelion ~4 July (152 million km).

Angle of rays

Direct rays travel less distance, cover less area → more insolation (low latitudes). Slanting rays → less (high latitudes).

Season cycle

Axial tilt + revolution shift the thermal equator. NH high‑sun: 21 Mar–23 Sep (summer solstice 21 June). SH high‑sun: 23 Sep–21 Mar (21 Dec).

Atmosphere

Scattering by aerosols, absorption by water vapour, reflection by clouds.

  • Latitudinal heat budget: low latitudes don't overheat because global circulation (atmospheric + gyre/ocean currents) transfers heat poleward.

Atmospheric temperature

Heat in the air = air temperature – fundamental element of weather; regulated by insolation and in turn regulates pressure. Principal source: outgoing long‑wave terrestrial radiation; incoming short‑wave solar radiation is secondary.

Controls: latitude · land–water · ocean currents & winds · cloud cover · altitude.

Land–water & temperature anomaly
  • Thermal zones assume uniform surface; actually water (mobile, translucent) moderates while land shows extremes.
  • Deviation from normal for a latitude = temperature anomaly: +ve (above normal) or –ve.
  • High‑sun season: land +ve vs water; low‑sun season: land –ve vs water.

Isotherms (lines of equal temperature) run parallel to latitudes but bend at coasts: summer isotherms bend towards equator going land → water; winter isotherms bend towards poles.

Currents, winds, clouds
  • Warm NAD + onshore westerlies keep European plains warmer than the Manchurian plains at the same latitude.
  • Seasonal example: blizzards (cold polar winds + powdery snow) give lower‑latitude USA polar winters.
  • Cloud cover: cloudy day → cooler; cloudy night → warmer.
Vertical distribution & inversion ★
  • Temperature falls with height at normal lapse rate 6.5 °C/km – moving away from the source (terrestrial radiation).
  • Tropopause: 18 km at equator (warm rising convection), 8 km at poles (subsiding). Due to NLR, the lowest tropopause temperature is over the equator.
  • Temperature inversion = temperature rising with height.

Air convergence

Near 60°, cold and warm air meet → front; warm air rises over cold → inversion; precondition for temperate cyclones.

Adiabatic changes

Temperature change from vertical movement: rising air cools, sinking air warms. Condensation releases latent heat → multi‑level inversions.

Air drainage

After sunset cold air drains from mountain walls to valley floor; next forenoon walls warm, valley cold → inversion. Walls favoured for settlement & farming. Strongest on long, clear, calm winter nights.
Air = poor conductor of heat. Fog = near‑ground cloud. Exam line: "Vertical distribution of temperature – write about inversion."

Pressure, winds & global circulation

Pressure systems
  • Pressure = weight of the air column on the surface; regulates and is regulated by global circulation.
  • LP systems – rising convection (thermal direct or indirect); troughs (large) and cyclones (small).
  • HP systems – subsiding convection; wedges (large) and anticyclones (small).
  • Mean sea‑level pressure = 1013.25 mb; studied horizontally in climatology.
  • LP → disturbances; HP → stability.
7 pressure belts
  • Continuous belts: both polar highs, SH sub‑polar low (homogeneous relief), equatorial low (rising convection gives similar wet conditions over land & water).
  • SH sub‑tropical high is near cellular; truly cellular = NH sub‑tropical high & sub‑polar low, due to unequal land–water.
  • High sun: tropical land gets +ve anomaly (LP) → STH survives as ocean cells – Hawaiian High (Pacific), Azores High (Atlantic).
  • Low sun: temperate land –ve anomaly → SPL survives as ocean cells – Aleutian Low (Pacific), Icelandic Low (Atlantic).
Wind (advection) & its forces
  • Convection creates pressure difference: rising → LP on ground/HP aloft; subsiding → HP on ground. Horizontal compensating flow = advection (wind), always HP → LP. HP = divergence, LP = convergence.
  • Classification – surface: prevailing, seasonal (monsoon), local; upper air: polar night jet, polar front jet, subtropical jet.

Pressure gradient force

Genesis of wind; sets direction (wind named after where it comes from) and speed – steeper barometric slope → faster wind.

Coriolis force

Ferrel's law: right in NH, left in SH. Affects only direction; increases with latitude and wind speed. 0° = 0%, 30° = 50%, 60° = 86.6%, 90° = 100%.

Friction

Surface drag changes direction & speed of surface winds. Ekman spiral: veering (clockwise), backing (anticlockwise).
Surface gradient wind = PGF + Coriolis + Friction. Geostrophic wind (upper air) = PGF + Coriolis. Geostrophic winds absent in equatorial belt.

Tri‑cellular meridional circulation

Each surface wind has an opposite upper‑air "anti‑wind".

Hadley cell (0–30°)

Thermal direct; rising warm air at equator, sinking at STH; NE trades + anti‑trades. Wet equator, dry sub‑tropics.

Ferrel cell (30–60°)

Thermal indirect; SW westerlies + anti‑westerlies; sinking at STH, rising at SPL. Wet temperate belt.

Polar cell (60–90°)

Thermal direct; sinking cold polar air; NE polar winds + anti‑polar; dry; lowest height.
Cross‑check: notes write the middle cell as "Flohn cell" – standard name is Ferrel cell.

Jet streams & monsoon ★

Jet streams (geostrophic flow)
  • Studied after 1945: strong westerlies near tropopause; no friction → geostrophic, parallel to isobars; form latitudinal garlands; fast cores = jet streams.
  • Notes: six garland systems globally except equator; jets "missing" in SH due to water dominance.
Cross‑check: subtropical and polar‑front jets exist in both hemispheres; SH jets are often stronger and more zonal.

Exceptional bend of ITCZ & TEJ
  • High‑sun season shifts the thermal equator/ITCZ north. Only over the northern Indian Ocean basin does continentality bend the ITCZ far north onto the northern plains of India.
  • This displaces the STJ from South & SE Asia, and a seasonal upper‑air easterly develops – the Tropical Easterly Jet (TEJ), regulator of the monsoon.
  • Winter: Hadley cell with STJ aloft and NE trades below. Summer: Modified Hadley cell with TEJ aloft and SW monsoon below.
Monsoon mechanism – classical

Arab scholars called the seasonal wind reversal "mausim".

Thermal concept (Halley)

Differential heating of land & water. Winter: STH over India → offshore NE trades, dry. Summer: LP over India → onshore SW monsoon, rain. Likened to land/sea breeze – fails to explain rain deep in the interior.

ITCZ concept (Flohn)

Continentality bends ITCZ to N India; SE trades cross the equator (SE monsoon in coastal Somalia & Yemen), get deflected between ~40–60°E and become the SW monsoon over India & Indo‑China. Also explains absence of west‑margin climate there.
Cross‑check: notes write "Hadley's thermal concept"; the classical thermal theory is by Edmond Halley (1686).
Modern: MONEX, Tibetan Plateau, vortices
  • Late‑1960s droughts → MONEX (monsoon experiment); results by mid‑1970s. Accepts ITCZ bend and SW monsoon = SE trades; adds Tibetan Plateau role and vortices.
  • Tibetan Plateau: ground LP marks northern limit of low pressure; HP builds vertically above → displaces STJ to Central Asia → Modified Hadley cell with TEJ = N–S alignment (IMD nomenclature). Keeps the gradient between Mascarene High and Tibetan Low for ~4 months, pulling SE trade branches as SW monsoon.
  • Different branches explain periodicity, unpredictability in amount, variable onset & retreat (variable moisture capacity).
  • Vortex development: LP over India (monsoon trough + Tibetan low) pulls the Indian Ocean equatorial trough north; it weakens and splits on the coast into LP cells – Malabar vortex (first showers on Malabar coast) and Utkal vortex (moist winds into Odisha plains). Vortex + monsoon trough → sudden burst of monsoon. Branches: Arabian Sea & Bay of Bengal.
ENSO & teleconnections
  • MONEX explained minor traits, not weakening/failure → explained by teleconnection with El Niño & Southern Oscillation in the Pacific (Walker).
  • Walker cell (normal): pressure pattern in S Pacific sustains strong SE trades → good SW monsoon.
  • El Niño: modified Walker cell weakens or reverses SE trades → weak monsoon.
  • Successful monsoon needs N–S alignment (Modified Hadley) + E–W alignment (Walker cell). La Niña = re‑established Peruvian current.

Local winds

Surface gradient winds that explain weather of specific places. 4 categories:

Land & sea breeze

Coastal differential heating: sea breeze by day, land breeze by night.

Katabatic & anabatic

Mountain & valley breezes: katabatic (downslope, night, air drainage); anabatic (upslope, day).

Depression winds

Cold‑dry polar outbreaks (winter) and warm winds with dust devils (high sun).

Descending winds

Adiabatically warmed downslope winds – favourable or unfavourable.

Moisture, condensation & precipitation

Humidity

Water vapour in the troposphere = moisture; in a given air column = humidity. Source: evaporation (WMO: includes transpiration & sublimation); rate depends on air temperature and humidity. Sequence: evaporation → condensation → precipitation.

MeasureUnitUse
Absoluteg/m³Not used climatologically – air volume keeps changing

Condensation forms

Supersaturated air + hygroscopic nuclei → condensation (vapour to liquid/solid). Cooling below dew point by contact cooling or adiabatic cooling.

At ground

Dew (above 0 °C, low latitude/altitude), frost (below 0 °C). Complete hydrological cycle without precipitation. Contact cooling, low‑sun season.

Near ground – Fog

Near‑surface cloud; cold dense air settling. Types: radiation (commonest), advection (European plains – warm moist westerlies over cold ground), frontal (mid‑latitude), precipitation fog (vapour added by frontal rain; "arctic smoke").

Visible aggregates of ice crystals & droplets; adiabatic cooling, all year. Polar regions: few clouds (air too dense to rise).

Precipitation – form & process
  • Precipitation = falling of moisture from cloud base; needs a raindrop‑forming process: merger of droplets and growth of ice crystals at the expense of droplets (ice has lower vapour pressure) – Bergeron process.
  • Rain (with drizzle = immature, small drops) – commonest in low–mid latitudes.
  • Snow (frozen form; mid–high latitudes) with sleet (partly melted snow, mid‑latitude winter) and glaze.
  • Hail – ice balls; need strong updraft → lower latitudes (not deserts); crop hazard.
Cross‑check: notes call glaze "crystallised snow"; standard: glaze = ice coating from freezing rain; sleet = ice pellets (US) or rain–snow mix (UK).

Stability & instability
  • NLR 6.5 °C/km ≠ ELR (actual rate of a given air column).
  • ALR = cooling rate of rising air: DALR 10 °C/km (unsaturated) and WALR ~5 °C/km (saturated; "retarded" by latent heat release).
  • Stability – subsiding air (HP, clear sky); ELR less than ALR → air stops rising.
  • Absolute instability – ELR > DALR: tornadoes, thunderstorms, tropical cyclones, convectional rain.
  • Conditional instability – WALR < ELR < DALR: forced rise in orographic & frontal conditions.
Hydrological cycles

Atmospheric

Falling moisture re‑evaporates (adiabatic warming) before reaching ground.

Daily

Equatorial: forenoon evaporation, afternoon rain – within 24 h.

Delayed / elongated

Moisture stored in biosphere and cryosphere (ice).

Air masses ★

  • 3‑D masses homogeneous in temperature & humidity, acquired from the surface; act as thermal engines of weather. Strongest in winter.
  • Source area – where properties are acquired; almost any surface except mountains. In source = barotropic; after advection and modification (conduction, convection) = baroclinic.
  • Classified by latitude (T, P), relief (c, m), temperature (k cold, w warm), stability (s, u) → 16 types. Globally 4 major ones used.
  • Europe has no truly cold air mass – always under warm NAD.

Cyclones ★

  • Also called extra‑tropical / wave cyclone; tied to the NH polar front (max contrast of air masses: warm westerlies vs cold polar winds).
  • Rain from sub‑tropics to sub‑polar areas; life 15–20 days (notes); no turbulent weather, not a hazard.
  • Polar front theory – Bjerknes: 6 stages.

1. Frontogenesis

Contrasting air meets near 60°; stable (stationary) front lasts long; air masses intrude into each other.

2. Division of front

Cold air pushes to lower latitude, meets LP on its left (Buys Ballot law) → cyclonic circulation begins.

3. Quick shift of cold front

Dense cold air rapidly takes ground.

4. Closing of warm sector

Warm air loses ground contact as it marches over cold air at higher latitude.

5. Occlusion

Cold air occupies the whole area; warm air lifted. Frontal decay begins, wet phase ends.

6. Frontolysis

Front dies; same cold air everywhere on ground.

Climatic classification: Köppen & Trewartha

Climate = aggregate of weather over large space & long time. Three approaches:

Genetic

One element, e.g. thermal or precipitation zones.

Empirical

Absolute temperature & precipitation values – Köppen, Trewartha.

Applied

Relative – temperature efficiency & precipitation effectiveness – Thornthwaite. More logical.
Köppen – level 1 (6 capital letters)

Inspired by De Candolle's vegetation classification; notes date Köppen's scheme to 1931 with altitude (H) added in 1936. Bases: temperature (A, C, D, E), precipitation (B), vegetation names.

B – Dry

Evaporation always exceeds precipitation. BW (BWh, BWk) and BS (BSh, BSk).

West margins under permanent STH; cT, offshore winds. Summer mT encroachment → up to 50 cm. Sahara best example; Atacama driest, with cold current effect.

BWk – mid‑latitude desert

Continental interiors; 0–20 cm. Kalahari, Patagonia (piedmont), Gurbantünggüt in China (intermontane).

Temperate continental; 25–75 cm, frontal, dependable. BSh (warmer): Prairies (USA), Pampas, Veld, Downs. BSk (cooler): Canadian Prairies, European steppes, Canterbury Plains (NZ).

C – Sub‑tropical

Controlled by warm anticyclones (STH cells).

Cs – Mediterranean

Sub‑tropical west margin; long hot dry summer; winter rain from onshore westerlies (mT); 50–75 cm. Mediterranean shores (not Egypt, Libya), central California.

Cf – European / wet west marine

Westerlies all year; 125–130 cm, wettest west margin. Cfb NW Europe warmer (NAD); Cfc British Columbia, S Chile (cold currents).

Cw – China type

Sub‑tropical east margin; up to 150 cm summer rain from onshore winds; N Chinese plains, Appalachian piedmont; weak polar outbreak gives minor winter rain; tropical cyclones in lower parts.

East margin sequence Am → Cw → Dw; west margin BWh → Cs → Cf.

Dw – Manchurian / Laurentian

Temperate east margin; up to 100 cm from onshore summer winds and polar outbreak fronts; winter restricted; Manchuria (NE China).

E – Polar & H – Montane
  • ET – Tundra: sub‑polar; brief summer melts surface ice over permafrost → waterlogged; up to 80°, northern limit Franz Josef Land; summer frontal rain ≈20 cm; only NH.
  • EF – Frost: Greenland & Antarctica; cold polar desert, absolutely stable; 6 months night, 6 months day.
  • Season cycle applies 10°–80°; none at equator & poles.
  • H – Montane (1936): depends on latitude, altitude, sun‑facing slopes (south in NH, north in SH), maritime influence (wet windward, dry leeward).
Trewartha & appraisal of Köppen
  • Trewartha (American): empirical; modified symbols; improved China‑type notation; less used than Köppen. Scheme: Ar, Aw, BWh/BWk/BS, Cs, Cfw, Do, Dc, E, Ft, Fi.
  • Köppen limitations: unclear/flexible symbols; absolute values; De Candolle symbols don't describe climate fully – 'f' covers 300 cm (Af) and ~10 cm (Df); a, b, c, d used in relative sense.
  • Thornthwaite's criticism: equatorial areas aren't the hottest despite high sun (heavy rain); similar rainfall yields deserts in tropics but forests in sub‑polar areas.

Thornthwaite 1948 – potential evapotranspiration
  • Published as "An Approach toward a Rational Classification of Climate". Introduced potential evapotranspiration (PE) – moisture that would evaporate/transpire if available; reveals real moisture left in air or soil.
  • Data for PE being scarce, he used 4 indices: humidity provinces (moisture index), seasonal variation of humidity, thermal efficiency regions, seasonal variation of thermal efficiency.

1. Humidity provinces (9)

A per‑humid · B4–B1 humid · C2 moist sub‑humid · C1 dry sub‑humid · D semi‑arid · E arid.

2. Seasonal humidity

Humid climates – aridity index: r (no deficit), s/w (minor summer/winter deficit), s2/w2 (major). Arid climates – humidity index: d (no surplus), s/w (minor winter/summer surplus), s2/w2 (major).

3. Thermal efficiency (9)

A′ megatherm · B′4–B′1 mesotherm · C′2–C′1 microtherm · D′ tundra · E′ frost.

4. Summer concentration of TE

a′ … d′: % of annual TE in summer; rises with latitude. Not applied to E′.

  • Methodological improvement over Köppen; best for applied climatology – agricultural planning (crop stand), water resources, regional, rural & urban planning; soil moisture and sub‑surface reservoirs.
