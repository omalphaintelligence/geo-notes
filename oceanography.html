Submarine topography

Oceanic crust = example of 1st‑order relief. The varied landforms on it = submarine topography, mostly 2nd‑order relief, since they lie below the base level of erosion.

  • Bathymetry = branch of oceanography that studies submarine topography.
  • Hypsometric curve = one graph showing onshore heights and offshore depths together. In practice used for offshore relief; it shows 3 major features: continental shelf → continental slope → deep sea (abyssal) plain.
Continental shelf
  • Immediate offshore feature = submerged part of the continent.
  • Formation linked to positive rejuvenation (tectonic + climatic factors).
  • Major shelves = result of sea‑level rise of up to ~200 m after the Pleistocene ice age → common shelf depth ≈ 200 m from MSL.
  • Depth variations: heavy deposition along passive margins → as shallow as 60 m; where submarine canyons exist → up to 600 m.
  • Width depends on height of onshore relief (nature of submergence): broad along coastal plains, narrow along tall mountains.
Margin note: Atlantic shoreline = passive margin.
Continental slope & continental rise
  • Transition between true granitic crust (shelf) and true basaltic crust (deep‑sea plain).
  • Starts at the shelf break: from ~200 m to ~3500 m.
  • Passive margin: foot deposits = continental rise. Material comes from continents but can't settle on the slope due to gradient → depositional 3rd‑order relief.
  • Active margin: at the slope foot a submarine trench forms by subduction of the heavier basaltic abyssal plain → no continental rise. Yet continent‑sourced material reaches the trench foot (deepest parts).
Submarine canyons (3rd‑order relief)

Found on shelves and slopes, below base level of erosion (Powell). Vertical abrasional feature explained by 2 schools:

① Developed first, inundated later

Classical; strictly uses base‑level concept. Via positive rejuvenation, gradation agents cut canyons before the platform was submerged. Examples: Hudson (Atlantic), Indus (Arabian Sea), Padma (Bay of Bengal). Explains canyons on shelves well.

② Turbidity currents

Modern; came up once canyons were found on slopes too. Subsurface water flow towards open sea, triggered by submarine quakes, tsunamis, strong backwash. Moves shelf deposits → abrasion agents called turbidites. Cuts canyons on shelf + slope, leaves fan deposits at the base. Still treats MSL as base level; turbidity currents are occasional.
Cross‑check: the Bay of Bengal canyon is commonly called "Swatch of No Ground".
Abyssal plains
  • True basaltic crust; holds both constructive and destructive features.
  • Spreading boundary → main mid‑ocean ridge (most important constructive feature; extensive volcanism, submarine mountains).
  • Transform faults → magma ejection builds ancillary ridges roughly perpendicular to the main ridge.
  • Isolated sea rises: conical seamounts, flat‑topped guyots.
  • These constructions divide the plain into variable‑sized basins.
  • Destructive: submarine trenches along subduction boundaries.
  • Fracture zones = transform faults without volcanism, also tied to basins.
Framework: Ridge (main / ancillary / sea rises) → Basin → Trenches + Fracture zones.
Answer checklist per ocean: shelf, slope, abyssal plain + marginal water bodies + names + islands.

Ocean‑wise submarine relief

  • 2nd largest ocean; best example of diverging boundaries – formed as N & S America detached from Angaraland/Laurasia & Gondwanaland.
  • Roughly "S"‑shaped; narrowest in equatorial belt, broadest near 50°S where it opens into the Southern Ocean.
  • Passive margins → extensive shelves, >13% of area → called a shelf‑water body.
  • Major shelves: NE – Dogger Bank; NW – Grand & Georges Banks; SW – Patagonian shelf. Northern basin has the most marginal water bodies.

Mid‑Atlantic Ridge
  • Dominant feature; avg depth ~2000 m; two parts: Dolphin (north) and Challenger (south).
  • Dolphin: Iceland → equator; narrow except Azores Plateau (broadest, mid‑latitude). Ancillary: Wyville Thomson Ridge & Telegraph Plateau – submarine boundary of Atlantic/Arctic. Splits N Atlantic into NE basin (Iberian) and NW basin (Caribbean).

  • Rio Grande Rise splits SW basin → Brazilian (N) & Argentine (S).
  • Fracture zone: Romanche at equator, separating Dolphin & Challenger.
  • Trench (minor plate, active margin): Puerto Rico Trench – Milwaukee Deep.

  • Continental: Caribbean islands, British Isles (north); Tierra del Fuego (south).
  • Volcanic on main ridge: Azores, Tristan da Cunha. On small sea rises: Canaries.
  • Plus coral and aggradational islands.

Eastern basin

  • Shelves & marginal seas confined to western side, between mainland and island arcs.

Salinity

Location & climate of oceans and their marginal seas decide both physical properties – salinity & temperature.

  • Salinity = amount of dissolved substances; sea water = universal solvent.
  • >47 salts; NaCl ≈ 77% – main source of salinity.
  • Measured in parts per thousand (ppt) / g per kg. Average 34–35 ppt.
5 controlling factors

Temperature / latitude

Higher temp → more evaporation → more salinity.

Precipitation

Dilutes water → wetter climates less saline than dry.

Ocean currents

West‑boundary warm currents → instability → lower salinity; east‑boundary cold currents → stable → higher.

Fresh water influx

River mouths & ablated (melt) water dilute strongly.

Continentality (10°–60° N/S)

Tropical marginal seas: +ve temp anomaly → more saline than open ocean. Temperate marginal: –ve anomaly → less saline.
Distribution tree

Horizontal → Latitudinal + Regional (boundaries, marginal water bodies)
Vertical → Pelagic, Halocline, Benthic

Aquatic environment has latitudinal, regional (open vs marginal) & vertical (depth) dimensions. Marginal water bodies: shelf, transitional, deep.

Latitudinal pattern (open oceans)

Except equatorial water, salinity falls as latitude rises.

Regional pattern
  • Open‑ocean boundaries: west boundaries always less saline than east at the same latitude.
  • Currents in S Atlantic & S Indian Oceans control even loosely‑bounded marginal seas: Rio de la Plata & Mozambique Channel < Walvis Bay & Timor Sea.
  • Sargasso Sea (gyre centre, N mid‑Atlantic) – very high salinity under anticyclonic conditions.
  • Tropical marginal seas > open ocean; range depends on continentality, climate, river mouths. Mediterranean, Arabian Sea, Gulf of California > Gulf of Mexico, Bay of Bengal, South China Sea. Gulf of Aqaba (IO) & Gulf of Sirte (Atlantic) are highly saline.
  • Temperate marginal seas < open ocean; salinity falls with continentality/fresh water; less variation than tropics. Seasonal melt + river water: Baltic vs Gulf of Bothnia.
  • Exception: North Sea (Atlantic) & Norwegian Sea (Arctic) are more saline than open water – warm NAD & Norwegian Current.
Red Sea = most saline (Indian Ocean). Baltic Sea = least saline (Atlantic).
Cross‑check: notes say Baltic is less saline than Gulf of Bothnia; standard texts say the Gulf of Bothnia (its northern arm) is the least saline part.
Vertical pattern – S‑diagram
  • All factors act only on the surface mixing zone = Pelagic zone (up to ~500 m).
  • Deep Benthic zone → uniform salinity irrespective of latitude/region.
  • Halocline (~500–1500 m) = compensating layer; S‑diagram shows both decrease and increase with depth.

Temperature & heat budget

Ocean water temperature
  • Amount of heat in a sample of sea water.
  • Main source: incoming solar radiation; secondary regional source: submarine volcanism.
  • Controlled by latitude, precipitation, boundary currents, continentality, influx of cold melt water.
  • Latitudinal: falls with latitude, except equatorial water.
  • Regional: western boundary warmer than eastern. Tropical marginal seas warmer than open ocean; temperate marginal seas colder – except North Sea & Norwegian Sea.
  • Vertical: all variation in surface mixing zone, homogeneous benthic zone. T‑diagram shows change in the intermediate thermocline. Over submarine volcanism: an extra thermocline in benthic zone with temperature rising with depth.
Ocean heat budget

Stored ocean energy is a major regulator of global (incl. atmospheric) circulation. Imbalance between incoming & outgoing radiation = heat flux; maximum at surface, falls with depth. For balance, net flux must be zero, depending on:

Incoming solar radiation

Varies with latitude, season and albedo (reflected energy, set by nature of water surface) → variable heating.

Outgoing IR radiation

Ideally all received heat; in practice varies with season (night length) and cloud cover.

Specific / sensible heat

Flux out by conduction to air; rate set by sea–air temperature contrast.

Latent heat

Flux out by evaporation; depends on air temperature and humidity.

Convection

Movement of water: latitudinal mix (gyre) + vertical mix (thermohaline).

Ocean currents

Ocean water mobility = current systems + tide‑generated movements. Currents → surface (gyre) and sub‑surface (thermohaline). Gyre = closed loop driven by prevailing winds: clockwise in NH, anticlockwise in SH.

Gyre circulation – parts
  • Equatorial water moves westward (earth's spin) → North & South Equatorial Currents (NEC, SEC). Shoreline barrier along doldrums → eastward Counter Equatorial Current.
  • Polar/sub‑polar water moves eastward: drag of westerlies + Coriolis deflection = Nansen effect.
  • Uninterrupted Southern Ocean → pure eastward West Wind Drift / Circum‑Antarctic Drift. In NH, continents make it a NE drift.
  • West‑flowing equatorial + east‑flowing polar water + boundary currents = closed gyre.
Boundary currents (geostrophic)
  • Latitudinal salinity differences → water‑level variations → divergence (equatorial, sub‑polar) & convergence (tropical, temperate) zones.
  • Ekman transport piles up water in convergence zones = Ekman pumping, mainly at ocean boundaries.
  • Trades → convergence at SW boundary (NH) & NW boundary (SH). Westerlies → NE boundary (NH) & SE boundary (SH).
  • Piled water → geostrophic currents (gravity flow + Coriolis). All boundary currents are geostrophic.

El Niño & La Niña
  • Linked to ocean size, distance between shorelines, and currents' effect on weather.
  • Peruvian Current is farthest from its western counterpart → coldest, thinnest east‑boundary current → driest weather (Atacama, ~12 mm rain/yr).
  • Dry east shore + wet west shore → water gradient → weak warm water drifts W → E, taking 9–10 months.
  • On reaching the east, it meets strongest upwelling, terminates the thin Peruvian Current and replaces it with warm water = El Niño; pressure gradient reverses → abnormal wet conditions on east shore.
  • Piled water + onshore winds + rain re‑establish Peruvian Current, gyre and normal weather = La Niña; more abrupt than El Niño.
Thermohaline circulation
  • Sub‑surface system covering all 3 layers: pelagic, pycnocline, benthic.
  • Most evident movement: downwell – cold dense surface water sinks to benthic zone; mixes vertically, carries dissolved O₂ & dead remains to depth → sustains deep habitats.
  • Downwelled water → benthic currents (horizontal, near abyssal plain). Little salinity/temperature contrast and no winds → missing or very slow (100s–1000s of years for a few degrees of latitude).
  • Near submarine volcanism / hydrothermal vents they warm → upwell → benthic water joins the surface gyre.
Equatorial + sub‑polar = divergence; tropical + temperate = convergence (surface movements).

Tides ★

Tide = bulge/pulse on the water surface from lunar & solar attraction. Bulge = tide; relatively depressed water = ebb. Two concepts: Equilibrium (Newton) and Dynamic (Laplace).

Equilibrium concept (Newton)
  • Assumes the earth's surface stays in equilibrium with tide‑generating forces – the bulge keeps pace with moon & sun.
  • Tides = planetary waves: every attractive bulge has an equal, opposite centrifugal bulge. Wavelength = half earth's circumference; height ≤ 2 m.
  • 3 types: lunar, solar, mixed.

Lunar tides

Stronger (moon is nearer). Tidal day is 52 min longer than earth's spin → gap between tides 12 h 26 min. Moon's declination 28.5°N–28.5°S → tropical (unequal successive highs/lows) & equatorial (steady levels) tides over 27.5 days. Axial tilt → attractive & centrifugal bulges felt in both hemispheres.

Solar tides

Same bulges but much weaker (distance). Sun's apparent shift between the tropics → tropical & equatorial tides over 365 days – less significant; mainly felt through mixed tides.

Dynamic concept (Laplace)
  • In reality the bulge can't keep pace with the moon. Earth's spin + shoreline barriers + Coriolis → consistent movements called tidal waves / tidal currents.
  • Southern (open) basins: bulge + spin → primary waves moving E → W, uninterrupted only in the Southern Ocean (Whewell – progressive waves).
  • Elsewhere in the south they hit western shores, pile up → gradient → fast derived waves; Coriolis deflects them left → W & NW flow, keeping water moving ~12 h after the moon crosses the longitude.
  • Northern basin: enclosed → higher bulge → Coriolis on primary waves → counter‑clockwise standing waves = amphidromic systems (Harris).
  • Amphidromic systems → higher tidal range → tidal energy harnessing mostly in the northern basin.

Marine deposits

Below base level of erosion (Powell), platforms hold lots of unconsolidated debris of varied texture, composition & origin – commercially important. Classified by texture (fine, coarse), source★ (terrigenous, biogenous, hydrogenous, cosmogenous), location (neritic, pelagic, benthic).

Source classification: Murray & Renard (1891), 4 categories.
Terrigenous
  • From continents: gradational material (largest share) + dead terrestrial organisms.
  • Sub‑classified by texture: coarse on shallow platforms, poorly sorted; fine in deeper areas, well sorted.
  • Well‑sorted → continental rise. Poorly‑sorted shelf deposits → turbidites, submarine erosion, and minerals: uranium – Walvis Bay, phosphorite – Peru–Chile basin.
  • Biotic part is grouped with biogenous sediments.
Biogenous & ooze
  • All sediments from organisms; split into neritic and open‑ocean (ooze).
  • Neritic: land‑sourced biogenous + excreta/remains from the richest ecosystem – the shelf (neritic zone).

  • CCD (Calcium Carbonate Compensation Depth): depth below which calcareous ooze is absent. Notes give max photic depth ~2000 m as CCD; reason – acidic water with no phytoplankton/photosynthesis.
  • Terrigenous + biogenous ≈ 99% of all marine sediments.
Cross‑check: standard value of CCD is ~4000–5000 m (shallower in Pacific, deeper in Atlantic); cause = cold, high‑pressure, CO₂‑rich deep water dissolving CaCO₃. Use the textbook figure in exams.
Hydrogenous (authigenic) & cosmogenous

Evaporites

Neritic platforms in dry climates: oolites (CaCO₃ nodules), NaCl – Persian Gulf, Red Sea, Gulf of Kutch.

Precipitates

Deeper platforms: manganese nodules, phosphorites, iron‑rich red clay.

Cosmogenous

Extra‑terrestrial / inter‑planetary dust; trace amount and so fine it stays as suspended load.

Authigenic = deposited where it is formed, sourced by ocean water itself.

  • Demarcates political & economic jurisdiction of maritime states, plus rights of landlocked countries of the rim region.

  • Baseline: joins major projecting peninsulas, respecting neighbours' interests; all distances measured from it.

Marine resources

Extractable resources: biotic (nektonic, planktonic, pearl, seal) and abiotic (energy, minerals).

Fisheries (nektonic = swimmers)
  • Fish = most prominent aquatic life‑style; commercially used in temperate waters.
  • Tropics lack commercial value: impure shoals + high oil content → costly harvest, less fit as direct food.
  • Biodiversity falls as latitude rises. Inter‑tidal zone = between shoreline & coastline.

Major fishing grounds (temperate):

  • NE Atlantic – Dogger Bank
  • NW Atlantic – Grand & Georges Banks
  • NE Pacific – California Current
  • NW Pacific – Japan (Kuroshio, circum‑Pacific belt)
  • Southern Ocean (most open ocean) – whale (storehouse of resource), krill (most ecologically & economically useful), white king fish (highest‑priced, "white gold fish")

Key fishing nations: Japan, Norway, USA, Russia.

Plankton, seaweed, pearl, seal
  • Plankton – phototrophs supporting the ecosystem; once indirect food, now direct. Harvested in fishing grounds + all upwelling zones.
  • Seaweed – traditionally a food‑processing input, now direct food; common in tropical inter‑tidal zones.
  • Pearl – tropical, high‑saline / less transparent water (river loads) where natural nuclei enter oysters: Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Tonkin, Gulf of Thailand, Red Sea.
  • Seal – cold waters: White Sea (Arctic marginal sea), Gulf of Alaska; skin for fur. Fur centres: Montreal (E Canada port) & St Petersburg (Gulf of Finland).

Theories

Two puzzles: reefs found deeper than 200 m, and reefs rising above water as islands.

Subsidence – Darwin & Dana

Tectonic; fixed sequence fringing → barrier → atoll as island subsides and reef grows up and outward. ✔ Explains reefs below 200 m. ✘ Not coral islands, not co‑existence of all 3 types; subsidence fits a specific island, not the whole tropical belt.

Glacial control – Daly

Climatic; used for the whole tropics. Pleistocene corals lived at >200 m; post‑glacial sea rise → any of 3 types per local conditions. Islands form by consolidation from littoral vegetation.
Pleistocene ice age ended ~10,000 yrs ago. Sundarbans named after Sundari trees (mangroves). Kyoto Protocol – linked to warming.
Coral bleaching
  • Reefs in tropical photic zones = one of the richest ecosystems, like equatorial rainforests.
  • Bleaching = de‑colourisation = decay of reefs, caused by rising water temperature (global warming) + water pollution.
  • Chemical weathering outpaces reef building; polyp habitat lost.
  • Fewer reefs → fewer phytoplankton/green plants (carbon sinks) → more acidic water → faster weathering: a cascade.
  • Response: artificial implantation of reefs – mainly Australia & India.
