Man–environment: the big picture

From the "great age of synthesis" till 1960, man–environment interrelation was the dominant approach of geography. It gave birth to physical and human geography and sub‑disciplines like demography, cultural and economic geography.

Social Darwinism – Ratzel

Ratzel's ideas
  • Friedrich Ratzel (German), disciple of Humboldt, strongly influenced by Darwin's ideas that dominated early‑20th‑century scholarship.
  • Integrated Darwinism into determinism → extended the classical deterministic phase into social Darwinism.
  • In his texts "Anthropogeographie" (human geography) and "Political Geography" he compared society and state with an organism that can grow or die.
  • Growth = increase of living space (Lebensraum) – expanding the area of political or economic control.
  • Following Humboldt's determinism: similar location → similar mode of life; society/state in a favourable location will be "superior", able to expand its living space.
Ratzel's legacy
  • Built one of the biggest disciple legacies, including scholars of other schools.
  • His training spread determinism to the USA, with Ellen Churchill Semple and Ellsworth Huntington as its strongest contributors.
Exam tip: in a neo‑environmentalism answer, don't write about Ratzel.

Neo‑environmentalism – Semple & Huntington

Ellen Churchill Semple
  • Monumental text "Influences of Geographic Environment" – extremely rigid deterministic ideas.
  • Rejected any comparison of human capacity with nature: "Man is a child of nature", which gives him all challenges and prospects.
  • Treated man as "dust of the environment" → beginning of neo‑environmentalism, considered more extreme than environmentalism.
Ellsworth Huntington
  • Monumental text "Civilization and Climate": climate is the environmental component that regulates human action; every civilisation is bound to a typical climate.
  • Concluded that tropical civilisations, though older, are less developed than temperate ones.
  • Developed the concept of climatic optima, integrating selective survival and selective migration in search of the optimum climate.

Neo‑determinism – Griffith Taylor

Stop‑and‑go determinism
  • Semple–Huntington neo‑environmentalism ignored human technological capacity and changed ways of living → human geographers withdrew support → decline of determinism began.
  • In this setting Griffith Taylor proposed neo‑determinism: nature guides man along a particular path and man has no choice but to follow it.
  • But man can manipulate movement on that path – accelerate, slow down or even stop.
  • Also called "stop‑and‑go determinism" – it recognised human capacity in the man–environment relation and revived the significance of determinism.

Cultural / social determinism

Present form of determinism
  • Present ideology of determinism; credited to American scholars Bowman, Ullman and James.
  • Treats nature as neutral; how strongly people feel nature's dictates depends on their level of cultural and technological growth.
  • Explains co‑existence today of cosmopolitan cultural identities and "fourth world" (tribal) communities.
  • Like probabilism, considered a neutral idea with practical validity.

Possibilism

Basic idea & origin
  • Human capacity is stronger than nature; nature plays the role of an advisor.
  • Linked to human technological capacity → much younger than determinism.
  • First formal possibilist idea – French historian Lucien Febvre; French geographers fitted it into geography.
  • Three phases: classical possibilism → neo‑possibilism → probabilism.
Classical possibilism
  • Founder of the French school, father of human geography – Paul Vidal de la Blache; contemporary of Ratzel; reacted to German dominance.
  • Number of available possibilities is multiplied by variations in human attitude, which come from cultural background.
  • Seen as rigid – it almost removed nature's role even as the possibility provider.
  • His early death left a weak disciple legacy: followers Jean Brunhes and Reclus followed Febvre's line more (nature = provider of possibilities).
  • American Barrows called human geography "human ecology" – study of the human struggle to survive; this further weakened the approach.
  • So classical possibilism quickly gave way to neo‑possibilism and probabilism.
Neo‑possibilism
  • American Carl O. Sauer: nature provides possibilities and man is free to choose.
  • But the range of possibilities is limited by the "price" man is willing to pay to overcome nature's dictates.
Probabilism
  • O.H.K. Spate: nature gives possibilities, man is free to choose, but some are more probable – usually the easiest one.
  • Like cultural determinism, a neutral idea with practical validity.
Cross‑check: notes call Spate American; O.H.K. Spate was a British‑born geographer who worked mainly in Australia (and India).

Quantitative revolution

Why it came

Man–environment studies had no specific method → self‑moulded approach, flexible conclusions, descriptive nature, weaker scientific orientation.

Quantitative revolution = spread of statistical techniques with a model/theory‑building tradition to make geography accurate, orderly and rational. Assumptions: man as a rational economic creature, nature as an isotropic plane. Linked to locational analysis in human geography.

Settlement geography used central values (mean etc.) and sampling.

Zenith phase (1960s)
  • Pillars: Berry, Chorley, Peter Haggett (British, per notes) and David Harvey.
  • Peter Haggett – author of "Models in Geography"; tested the major human‑geography theories: Demographic Transition Model, Rank‑Size Rule, quantitative delimitation of the zone of overlap in urban spheres of influence.
  • Others: Hoover, Isard – industrial location models; Weaver – crop combination regions.
  • All branches quantified: standard deviation, regression, correlation; sampling → inductive reasoning with remote sensing data → GIS (computer handling of geographic data). Better data collection, processing, interpretation, maps, resource analysis, planning.
Cross‑check: "Models in Geography" (1967) was edited by Chorley & Haggett; Haggett's own classic is "Locational Analysis in Human Geography". David Harvey is British‑born (notes say American); Brian Berry is British‑born, worked in the USA.
Decline (early 1970s)
  • De‑humanisation – normative questions of human behaviour, beliefs, fear ignored as they can't be quantified.
  • Ever more complex data needed advanced maths that average geographers lacked.
  • Over‑generalisation of man–environment and reliance on "golden‑calf" computers.
  • David Harvey himself discouraged disciples – the revolution had "lived its course" with diminishing returns.
  • Result: declined in early 1970s, survives as one of many methods.
