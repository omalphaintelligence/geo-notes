22.1 Ice & snow line

Glacier basics
  • Glacier = ice mass moving downslope under gravity.
  • Today ~10% of earth's surface is glaciated; about 2–3 million years BP glaciers covered nearly one‑third of the land surface.
  • Glaciers form where ice accumulates above the snow line in extreme cold. World estimate: 70,000–200,000 glaciers.
  • Snow fields = areas of huge ice accumulation that feed glaciers.
Snow line
  • Boundary between permanent and seasonal snow – height above which snow cover is permanent (average temperature of warmest month stays below freezing).
  • Lowest (0 m, sea level) in polar regions; rises equatorward to 5000–6000 m.
  • Highest (~6500 m) in the drier parts of the Tibetan Plateau and Andes – dryness, not just latitude, matters.
Snow → glacial ice
  • Snow – fluffy, loosely packed flakes, very low density, open feathery look.
  • Weight of overlying snow compacts it into denser granular snow = firn / névé.
  • Further compaction → pure solid glacial ice.

22.2 Types of glaciers

Ahlmann's classification (1953)

Broadly 2 types: mountain/valley and continental. H.W. Ahlmann (1953) used thermal & morphological traits: 3 broad groups, 11 sub‑types.

Ice sheets, ice caps & continental glaciers
  • Ice sheets – biggest glaciers; broad domes, flattened cross‑section, thousands of km² and hundreds of km wide; bury the topography; ice radiates outward from the centre.
  • Difference is area: dome >50,000 km² = ice sheet; smaller = ice cap. Central part = ice dome.
  • Continental glaciers = extensive ice sheets covering most of a continent. Pleistocene sheets spread from Labrador and Keewatin centres over about half of N America.
  • Today: Antarctic (avg thickness ~4000 m; rises ~4500 m above MSL) and Greenland (~3000 m thick at central dome; covers ~¾ of the island). Smaller ice caps: Arctic Canada, Iceland, Norway.
Cross‑check: book gives Antarctic sheet ~8 million km² and Greenland ~1,30,000 km². Current figures: Antarctica ~14 million km², Greenland ~1.7 million km².

Piedmont, shelf, field, cirque & niche

Piedmont glacier

Several valley glaciers merge in the foothills; only in cold areas (they melt in warm foothills). Malaspina (Alaska).

Ice shelf

Floating thick ice sheet attached to the coast; no bed friction so it spreads freely. Ross, Ronne‑Filchner (Antarctica).

Ice field

Fairly flat, extensive ice mass.

Cirque glacier

Ice in an armchair‑shaped cirque.

Niche glacier

Small upland ice mass resting on a sloping rock face.

22.3 Movement of glaciers

Why and how glaciers move
  • Driven by gravity, which depends on ice thickness and bed gradient; ice "deforms to stress set up by gravity".
  • Internal stress: hydrostatic pressure (weight of ice, equal in all directions) and shear stress (from weight + surface slope) – shear makes ice particles slip past each other.
  • 3 ways: basal sliding over bedrock, internal deformation (creep), alternate compression and extension.
Rate of movement
  • Thicker ice + steeper slope → faster. Fastest at the centre line, slower at the sides; faster at surface than at the bed (wall/bed friction).
  • Average speed: 3–300 m per year. Internal deformation makes glaciers sluggish; basal sliding speeds them up.
GlacierSpeed
Meserve (Victoria Land, Antarctica)3–4 m/yr
Franz Josef (New Zealand)300 m/yr
Jakobshavn Isbræ (Greenland)7–12 km/yr
  • Periodic glacial surge – sudden rise to 4–7 km/yr; ice can move ~5 m per hour.
  • Forward movement = advancement; withdrawal = retreat; melting of the front with rising temperature = ablation.

22.4–22.5 Erosional work, U‑shaped & hanging valleys

Two views on glacial erosion

Protectionists

Glacier protects bedrock by covering it; ice is softer than rock so it can't erode.

Active‑agent school

Glaciers are potent agents of erosion and deposition; on average they modify pre‑existing landscapes.
  • Glacierization = coverage of an area by moving ice. Glaciation = action of moving ice on land (may include occupation + erosion + deposition).
  • Mechanisms: abrasion, plucking, polishing. Pure ice is inactive; debris carried at the base acts as tools of abrasion. Plucking = detaching large jointed blocks.
  • Erosional landforms: bumps (whaleback, rock drumlin), depressions, U‑shaped valleys, hanging valleys, cirques, arêtes, horns, nunataks, crag & tail, glacial stairways, roches moutonnées, trough lakes, tarns, fiords.
U‑shaped valleys
  • Glacial troughs: steep valley walls (concave slope) and broad flat floor; often with tributary hanging valleys.
  • View 1: glaciers flow through pre‑existing river valleys and reshape them by abrasion & plucking.
  • View 2 (extreme glacial erosion): glaciers dig their own valleys.
  • Given their size, conclusion: U‑valleys are modified pre‑existing valleys.
Hanging valleys

Tributary glacier valleys joining a much deeper main valley; after deglaciation their meltwater falls as waterfalls.

Glacial erosion school

Carved entirely by ice; differential over‑deepening – the bigger, longer main glacier erodes more, so tributaries hang.

Glacial protection school

Main valleys deepened by water; tributaries full of ice were protected, so they stayed high. Evidence: not all tributaries hang uniformly; not all glacial valleys have hanging valleys; hanging valleys occur only where tributary glaciers start higher than the main one.

Cirques, tarns, cols, arêtes & horns

Cirque – shape, names, parts
  • Armchair / amphitheatre‑shaped, horseshoe, steep‑walled depression at the head of a glaciated valley.
  • Names: cwm (Wales), corrie (Scotland), kar (Germany), botn (Norway).
  • Types: simple, compound, hanging, nivation cirques.
ComponentFeature
Head wallNear vertical, 600–900 m high; no talus at base → weathering plays no role, glacial erosion does
BasinFloor; may hold a lake after deglaciation
ThresholdOuter margin (lip)

Favourable conditions: widely spaced river valleys before glaciation; widespread heavy snowfall to build snow fields; homogeneous lithology.

Tarn, col, arête, horn
  • Tarn – rock basin on the cirque floor (thick ice, high pressure) filled with meltwater = cirque lake.
  • Partly glaciated upland = scalloped upland / biscuit‑board topography; fully dissected by cirque recession = fretted upland.
  • Arête – saw‑toothed, sharpened divide between cirques; a series of cols and ridge segments; called serrate range in England. Converging arêtes look like a star → star‑fish arêtes.
  • Col – gap where two cirque head walls intersect.
  • Horn – pyramidal peak from recession of 3+ cirques; named after Matterhorn (Switzerland). Crest‑line of arêtes and horns = col‑and‑peak topography.
  • Horn examples: Godwin Austen (K2), Broad Peak (Karakoram); Badrinath, Nanda Devi (Uttarakhand); Kailash (Tibet); Everest, Makalu (Nepal).

Other erosional landforms

Nunatak & crag and tail
  • Nunataks – peaks/mounds surrounded by ice on all sides; look like islands in an ice sea → "glacial islands"; shrink by lateral erosion and frost action.
  • Crag and tail – resistant volcanic/basaltic plug: steep eroded up‑glacier side (crag) and a gentle, long down‑glacier tail sheltered from ice.
Roches moutonnées
  • Streamlined asymmetrical hillocks: smooth gentle stoss (onset) side facing the ice, steep craggy lee side.
  • Term first used by de Saussure (1804) – looks like a sheep lying down.
  • Stoss side smoothed by abrasion & polishing; lee side steepened by plucking of joint blocks.
  • Scale: ~1 m (small), hundreds of metres (often along structural weakness), whole hills (largest). Common in the glaciated valleys of Kashmir Himalaya.
Glacial stairways, paternoster lakes, grooves
  • Glacial stairways (giant / cyclopean stairs) – steps from a few metres to several km long, separated by vertical cliffs 30–300 m; formed by abrasion and plucking of step faults across the glacier path.
  • Hollows at the foot of the steps fill with meltwater = paternoster lakes, which look like beads ("beaded lakes").
  • Glacial grooves – small streamlined depressions; up to 12 km long, 100 m wide, 30 m deep.
Fiords
  • U‑shaped glacial troughs occupied by the sea; steep walls, many hanging valleys; dug below sea level by valley glaciers from coastal mountains.
  • Deep near the land, shallower seaward: a raised threshold (some say a submerged terminal moraine) separates fiord and sea.
  • Found in: Scotland, Norway, Greenland, Labrador, British Columbia, Alaska, Patagonia, New Zealand – areas of dissected coastal plateaus and mountains.
  • Distribution conditions (A. Holmes & Doris L. Holmes, 1978): tectonic upland structures near the sea; pre‑existing valleys following them; heavy glaciation by thick seaward‑moving ice.
ViewOrigin
1Valleys carved above sea level, submerged later
2Eroded below sea level when Pleistocene sea level was low; river valleys over‑deepened, then drowned as ice melted
3Tectonic – grabens formed by faulting of the coastal land, then submerged

22.6–22.7 Glacial drift & moraines

Glacial drift & till
  • All rock debris carried by glaciers = glacial drift: till, ice‑contact stratified drift, outwash.
  • Moraine – used for both the debris and the landforms; properly a term for depositional landforms of direct glacial origin.
  • Till = unsorted, unstratified drift (also called boulder clay): basal/lodgement till (compact, dense, clay‑rich, laid at the base) and ablation till (loose, little fine material).
  • Ice‑contact stratified drift = glacial debris reworked by meltwater.
Debris by locationWhere
EnglacialInside the glacier
SupraglacialOn the surface
SubglacialAt the base, moved by touching the bed
Moraines – 4 locational types

Ridge‑like deposits of till; long, narrow, often higher than 30 m.

Terminal (end) & recessional

Across the snout after ablation; horseshoe/crescent ridges facing the valley; hundreds of km long, >100 m high. Retreat leaves irregular ridges and basins = knob‑and‑basin topography.

Lateral

Parallel ridges along both sides of the glacier as it shrinks; long, narrow, steep; hundreds of metres high.

Medial

Along inner margins of two glaciers at their confluence; stand above the valley glacier surface.

Ground

Till spread on the valley floor; unsorted mix of ablation till and stratified drift.

Drumlins

Shape, size, distribution
  • Swarms of rounded till hummocks shaped like an inverted boat or spoon; "elliptical hills, blunt on the up‑glacier end with an elongate down‑glacier tail" (A. Bloom, 1978).
  • A few m to 60–100 m high; a few hundred m to 1–2 km long. Clusters in regular pattern = basket‑of‑eggs topography.
  • Found in Finland, Northern Ireland, Wisconsin (~10,000 drumlins), middle New York, southern Michigan. Drainage becomes irregular among them.
DrumlinRoche moutonnée
OriginDepositional (boulder clay)Erosional
Steep sideUp‑glacierDown‑glacier (lee)
Theories of origin
  1. Glaciers re‑shape older terminal/recessional moraines in the next glacial period – erode the up‑glacier side, deposit on the down‑glacier side.
  2. Overloaded ice drops till below it; mounds obstruct flow and grow.
  3. Fluvial view: boulder‑clay mounds of the glacial period re‑shaped by water erosion in the interglacial.
  4. Leverett: formed by deposition of till by glaciers.

22.8 Glacio‑fluvial deposits & landforms

Ablation & meltwater deposits

Below the snow line the snout melts (ablation); meltwater streams carry sediment and drop it as fans (on land) or deltas (in water) → glacio‑fluvial deposits. Landforms: eskers, kames, kame terraces, kettles, outwash plains.

Eskers
  • Long, narrow, sinuous, steep‑sided ridges of sand and gravel in the middle of ground moraines; few m to tens of m high, km long, parallel to former ice flow; cross valleys, swamps, lakes – good routes for roads.
  • Origin: (1) channel from the snout gets obstructed and drops sediment; (2) supraglacial channel sediment settles on the floor when ice melts; (3) subglacial tunnels fill with sediment, seen after the ice melts.
  • Beaded eskers – swellings at regular intervals along an esker.
Kames, kettles, outwash

Kames

Mounds of bedded sand and gravel at the edge of retreating ice; cone kames (on land) and delta kames (in lakes). Kame terraces – flat‑topped ridges between glacier and valley side. Moulin / perforation kames – from holes in decaying ice.

Kettles & hummocks

Kettles = depressions in outwash plains from melting of large ice blocks; low mounds dotting large kettles = hummocks.

Outwash plain

Meltwater flows through the terminal moraine as sheet water, erodes it and deposits well‑sorted sediment in front; coalescing alluvial fans. Icelandic "sandar"; multi‑thread channels = braids.
