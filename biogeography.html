Diversity is studied at three levels: genetic, species, ecosystem.

Principles of ecology

Biosphere & Odum's hierarchy
  • Biosphere = narrow life zone where lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere meet; complex mix of habitats and links.
  • Odum: 6 levels of biological systems – gene → cell → organ → organism → population → ecological system (biotic community + abiotic). At every level biotic parts interact with abiotic parts to produce food, energy and matter.
Components of an ecosystem

Autotrophs (producers)

Chemotrophs – chemosynthesis, no sunlight; self‑feeders that can't support heterotrophs. Phototrophs – green plants, photosynthesis; primary producers supporting consumers.

Heterotrophs (consumers)

Primary consumers (herbivores), secondary consumers (carnivores), decomposers.

Abiotic

Inorganic – sunlight, water, soil/water nutrients; organic – carbohydrates, fats.

Ecosystem linkages & productivity

Abiotic → biotic: production

Producers + abiotic parts make food, energy, matter (chemosynthesis & mainly photosynthesis). Controls: sunlight, water (major); season cycle, age of vegetation (minor).

Biotic → biotic: productivity & food pyramid
  • Productivity = stored energy per unit time and area; in biogeography dry g/m²/yr. Biosphere average ~320; wet tropics up to ~2000; deserts as low as ~3.
  • Primary (producers) and secondary (consumers); each gross or net (gross minus respiration loss).
  • Feeding/ecological capacity falls up the trophic levels; pyramid has only 4–5 levels; largest biomass at level 1 (phototrophs).
  • Relations: prey–predator, parasitism (one gains, one loses); symbiosis – commensalism (one gains, other neutral), mutualism (both gain); competition (within a trophic level) – inter‑specific weak (goat vs giraffe), intra‑specific strong (goat vs goat).
Biotic → abiotic: decomposers

Decomposers turn excreta and dead remains into abiotic matter, completing the nutrient cycle: humification (making humus) → mineralisation (back to soil nutrients).

Terrestrial biomes

Biomes split by 1st‑order relief into terrestrial and aquatic. Terrestrial ones depend on climate (sunlight + rain → production). Less productive: desert, tundra; productive: forest, savanna, grassland. Sunlight is the prime regulator – so desert is more productive than tundra. Biodiversity falls with latitude.

Tundra
  • Sub‑polar; long cold winters, short summers; surface ice melts over permafrost → waterlogged. ~20 cm summer warm‑front rain (NH).
  • Very short growing season – floating plants, mosses, lichens. Animals migrate in summer: insects, birds (Siberian crane), polar bear, antelope (NH); penguins in SH.
Desert
  • Limited by water; wide habitat range but low productivity → maximum niche diversity, low biodiversity.
  • Xerophytes: deep roots (phreatophytes), water‑storing succulents – mesquite, aloe vera, thorny bushes, cactus, sabai grass, acacia, senegal; date palm in oases.
  • Animals: thick skin in cold plateau deserts (Tibetan yak, antelope, gazelle); in hot deserts big ears and nocturnal habit – camels, rodents, kangaroo rats.
Forest biomes

Most productive; >70% of the biosphere's biomass.

Mediterranean (sclerophyll)

Adapted to long hot dry summers: short stunted evergreen trees & bushes, deep roots, small thick glossy leaves. Citrus, fig, olive, maquis, chaparral (California). Small animals – rabbits, foxes.

Grassland
  • Temperate continental interiors; 25–75 cm rain – enough for grass, not trees.
  • Steppes – short grass (10–20 cm), semi‑arid (25–50 cm). Prairies – taller grass (40–60 cm), sub‑humid (50–75 cm). Notes' examples: N American prairies, Eurasian steppes, Veld, Downs, Pampas, Canterbury plains.
  • Grazers only: squirrels, rabbits, prairie dogs, bison (largest temperate herbivore).
Savanna
  • Tropical continental interiors; transition forest ↔ desert; "woodland" of trees, xerophytes and tall grasses (1.8–3 m: bamboos, runners, tufts).
  • Vertical feeding pattern → maximum animal diversity → "abode of wildlife"; most protected areas. Elephants, tigers, zebras, giraffes, buffaloes, monkeys; Australian marsupials – kangaroo, koala, platypus.
  • Ecotone = transition zone between two biomes.
Extreme adaptability: tundra, taiga, desert, ERF. Answer frame for any biome: location, climate, phototrophs, heterotrophs, soil.

Aquatic ecosystem

Basics & habitats
  • Controlled by sunlight, dissolved oxygen, nutrients; 3‑D (latitude, region, depth). Production depends on depth; respiration does not.
  • Life‑styles: plankton (drifting), nekton (swimming), benthos (attached).
  • Photic (limnetic) vs aphotic (profundal). Critical depth – where production = respiration; above it more biodiversity. Bloom = spring of aquatic life near this depth.
  • Pelagic (off the bottom) vs benthic (on the bottom).
Cross‑check: notes put max photic/critical depth at ~2000 m; usually the photic zone is ~200 m deep.
Fresh water
  • Fewer nutrients than sea. Running water less productive (current limits plankton); in middle & lower courses; always photic. Standing water (ponds, lakes, reservoirs) has clear limnetic & profundal levels. Lower latitudes more productive in both.
Marine
  • Horizontal: intertidal, neritic, oceanic. Vertical: pelagic – epipelagic (200 m), mesopelagic (1000 m), bathypelagic (4000 m); benthic; hadal (>4000 m).
  • Intertidal (littoral): on‑shore – coconut palm, white & screw pines (shallow roots, tall, crown leaves); off‑shore – mangroves, sundari, nipa (dense roots, short). Seaweeds give most diversity.
  • Neritic zone and upwelling zones = most productive (photic + land nutrients); safe breeding grounds. Epipelagic most productive oceanic layer.
  • Hydrothermal vents in benthic zone – chemosynthesis by chemotrophs (worms, clams).

World soil types

Zonal soils

Controlled by climate and its processes: pedocal (Ca‑rich), pedalfer (Al‑Fe rich), tundra.

GroupSoils
PedocalDesert soil – coarse, mineral‑rich, humus‑less, responds to inputs. Chernozem (steppe) – most fertile natural soil; fullest A1 → "black earth"; world's bread baskets. Chestnut – weaker chernozem
Long‑grass (transitional)Prairie soil (weaker calcification, less fertile than chernozem); red‑yellow savanna soil (no calcification, some leaching/laterisation)
Pedalfer (forest)Laterite (ERF; leaching + laterisation, acidic, OO layer); podzol (taiga; weak decomposition, podzolisation); podzolic (hardwood deciduous; most widespread fertile forest soil – monsoon podzolic less fertile than temperate)
TundraTechnically not a soil (no weathering); seasonal waterlogging supports floating plants

Fertility order: steppe > prairie > red‑yellow > podzolic.

Intrazonal & azonal soils
  • Intrazonal – infertile: halomorphic (saline‑alkaline from excessive calcification: Etosha Pan – Namibia, Makgadikgadi – Botswana, Rann of Kutch, Australian salt crust); hydromorphic (waterlogged: Mackenzie, Ob, Lena lower courses) incl. peat/bog via gleying – Pantanal, Gran Chaco, Pripet Marshes, Okavango.
  • Intrazonal – fertile (calcimorphic): regur (on basalt; "cotton soil": Borborema, Kordofan, Ethiopian Highlands, Deccan); rendzina (on limestone; humus‑rich: Anatolia, Sarawak, Sabah); brown forest soil (wet monsoon margins; young, coarse, shallow).
  • Azonal: montane ("scree soils", well drained, humus‑rich, shallow) and gradational (no horizons) in rising fertility: glacial < loess < alluvial. Loess – NW Hwang Ho (China), Adobe (Sacramento, USA), Limon (German–Belgian upland).
  • Alluvial fertility varies with texture (deltaic clay > flood plain), process (pedalfer Amazon < pedocal Nile), composition (red alluvium Amazon < black alluvium Nile).

Human impact on environment

Since the industrial revolution humans modify or destroy natural systems: atmosphere (GHGs, ozone depletion → climate change), hydrosphere (pollution, eutrophication, channelisation), biosphere (desertification, deforestation, biodiversity loss).

Climate change – natural causes
  • Solar output variation – notes: solar flares → warmer, sunspot phases → colder.
  • Milankovitch cycle – eccentricity (orbit shape; notes ~91,000 yrs), obliquity (tilt 22.1°–24.5°; notes ~42,000 yrs; "wobbling"), precession (notes ~12,000 yrs: axis points to Vega instead of Polaris, NH summer in December).
  • Volcanic dust – ash acts as aerosol, cools; notes link Tertiary vulcanism to the Pleistocene ice age and Icelandic ash to Europe's cold winters of 2010–11.
  • Continental drift – regional climate change.
Cross‑check: more sunspots actually mean slightly higher solar output; standard Milankovitch periods ≈100,000 / 41,000 / 23,000–26,000 years.
Human‑induced factors (IPCC)
  • IPCC (UNEP + WMO): present change is human‑induced – long‑lived GHGs CO₂, CH₄, N₂O; ozone‑depleting halogenated hydrocarbons.
  • AR4 (2007): 11 of 12 years in 1995–2006 among warmest on record.
  • AR3 "climate sensitivity": by 2100 surface temperature could rise up to 5.8 °C above ~15 °C; sea level up to 0.9 m; oceans absorb >90% of added heat (to ~3000 m).
Hydrosphere impact
  • Water quality: visible pollutants (developing world – Amazon, Ganga, Chang Jiang, Indus, Nile) vs invisible (developed world's "clean‑looking" rivers – Mississippi–Missouri, Danube, Rhine, Thames).
  • Eutrophication – nutrient overload blooms block light; IUCN list: Fort Peck & Sakakawea (USA), Kainji (Nigeria), Nasser (Egypt), Zeya (Russia), Dongting & Poyang (China).
  • IUCN: >95% of discharge untreated in developing world; 70–75% treated in developed. Oil spills, nuclear dumping = disasters in the making.
  • Channelisation: Amu & Syr Darya diverted (Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, Kazakhstan) → Aral Sea nearly dead, region desertifying; lower Amazon tamed up to Manaus → ERF thinning, habitat loss.
Biosphere impact
  • CBD lists 5 causes of biodiversity loss: over‑exploitation, habitat destruction, invasive alien species, climate change, nutrient overload & pollution.
  • Deforestation: temperate hardwood forests lost most area; ERF lost most diversity. Hunting controlled via IUCN Red Book conventions.
  • Desertification (UNCCD): biotic potential destroyed → desert‑like land; causes – deforestation, unsustainable farming, over‑grazing. Sahel (Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Chad) most desertified.
  • Invasive species: rabbits in Australia, eucalyptus in India (social forestry).

UNFCCC & Kyoto
  • Umbrella convention on GHGs; in force 1994; voluntary target – 1990 levels by 2000.
  • Annex 1 (industrial + transition economies – cut emissions), Annex 2 (developed – cut + fund low‑carbon tech), others (developing – no cuts).
  • COP3, 1997 – Kyoto Protocol: notes 6–8% below 1990 by 2008–12; CDM. COP6 part II, Bonn 2001 – flexible mechanism (carbon sinks, buying credits); carbon trading with CERs.
  • Kyoto implemented 2005 (COP11/MOP1, Montreal). COP15 Copenhagen – TEEB & REDD+; COP16 Cancún – MRV. COP18 Doha 2012 – 2nd commitment period (8 years).
Cross‑check: Kyoto's overall target was ~5.2% below 1990 (country targets varied).
Vienna Convention & Montreal Protocol

Vienna Convention 1985 → Montreal Protocol signed 1987, in force 1989; legally binding phase‑out of ODS; multilateral fund (UNEP, UNDP, UNIDO, World Bank). IPCC AR4: ozone has stabilised – a success.

CBD & its protocols
  • From Earth Summit; in force 1993; genetic, species, ecosystem diversity. Objectives: conservation, sustainable use, fair & equitable benefit sharing. COPs every 2 years; COP9 Bonn 2008 – the 5 threats.
  • Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety (2000) – safe handling of LMOs; advance informed agreement; Biosafety Clearing House.
  • Nagoya Protocol (COP10, 2010) – access to genetic resources & benefit sharing. Aichi targets – 5 strategic goals (mainstreaming, reduce pressures, improve status, enhance benefits, participatory planning). COP11 Hyderabad – "Hyderabad pledge".
  • 2004 Programme of Work on Protected Areas – ~6000 new PAs (~13% land, 6% aquatic).
Cross‑check: Aichi targets were for 2011–2020; COP11 Hyderabad was in 2012.
CITES, CMS, Ramsar
  • CITES – from an IUCN resolution, in force 1975; protects Red‑listed species from international trade (~30,000 per notes). Red Book: extinct, extinct in wild; critical, endangered, threatened; least concern.
  • CMS / Bonn Convention – migratory species: MoUs (Siberian crane, 7 marine turtles incl. olive ridley) and binding agreements (European bats, gorillas, African‑Eurasian waterbirds).
  • Ramsar (wetlands) – national action with international cooperation; ~2000 sites (notes).
Cross‑check: CMS in force 1983 (notes 1985); Ramsar signed 1971 (notes 1981); Ramsar sites now ~2500.
