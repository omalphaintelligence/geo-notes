Relief orders & chronological relief

Three orders of relief
OrderFeaturesCause
1stContinental crust (7 continents) & oceanic crust (5 ocean floors)Cooling & solidification of crust ~4000 Ma; later modified by drift
2ndMountains, plateaus (CC); mid‑oceanic ridges, trenches (OC)Endogenic forces from inside – "variability developers" (mountain building, vulcanism)
3rdValleys (erosional), deltas (depositional)Exogenic forces on the surface – "levellers" that erode, transport, deposit
  • Exogenic agents: rivers, wind, glaciers, sea waves, underground water – effective only on CC.
  • 3rd‑order relief is generally absent on the ocean floor because MSL is the base level of erosion; climate‑driven base‑level changes create some along continental margins (shelves).
  • Aravallis = old fold mountains; Himalayas = new (young) fold mountains.
Chronological development of relief
CategoryAgeFeatures & examples

First three categories are 2nd‑order relief. Young relief is the best understood and is used to interpret older relief.

Earth's interior & earthquakes

Compositional layers

Gradual cooling, solidification, degassing and sorting produced 3 concentric layers.

LayerDepthCompositionKey points
Crust0–60 km (Moho)SiAl (silica + alumina)Lightest; Conrad discontinuity ~30 km splits outer lighter & inner denser SiAl; all of crust is in brittle lithosphere
Mantle60–2900 km (Moho → Gutenberg)SiMa (silica + magnesium)~80% of earth's volume; Repetti discontinuity ~650 km splits outer lighter & inner denser SiMa
Core2900–6370 kmNiFe (nickel + iron)Densest; outer core liquid (radioactive heat) → geomagnetism; inner core solid due to pressure

Discontinuities: major – Moho, Gutenberg; minor – Conrad, Repetti, Lehmann.

Cross‑check: notes put Lehmann discontinuity (outer/inner core) at ~4300 km; standard value is ~5150 km.

Density rises steadily with depth; temperature rises too, but its rate of increase falls with depth. Asthenosphere & D″ = low velocity zones for seismic waves.

Earthquakes & seismic waves
  • Earthquake = sudden tremors from release of energy stored in deformed rocks. Focus = point of release inside (plutonic); epicentre = point on surface vertically above, maximum impact.
  • Sudden endogenic force that creates no 2nd‑order relief, but is a window to the interior. Richter scale – 1935.
WaveTypeSpeed (notes)Behaviour
P (primary)Body, compressional~7 km/s, fastestThrough all media; slows in liquid
S (secondary)Body, distortional (shear)~3.5 km/sNot through liquids → proves liquid outer core
LoveSurface≤2.5 km/sHorizontal ground shift (sweeping motion)
RayleighSurface≤2.5 km/sVertical ground shift (sea‑swell motion); submarine ones → tsunamis
  • Shadow zones: S‑waves absent over the opposite half of the earth (stop at Gutenberg, 2900 km); P‑wave shadow between 103° and 143° from the focus due to refraction.
  • Surface waves cause tremors and secondary hazards: landslides, avalanches, liquefaction.
  • Tsunami = seismic sea wave moving the whole water mass; in open sea wavelength 150–160 km, height ≤3 m, speed up to 800 km/h; near shore slows and rises up to 30 m. In marginal seas they are called seiches.

Geological time scale

Relative time

Age of rocks relative to neighbouring strata; credited to Nicolas (Steno) – "chronology of succession". 4 principles:

Superposition

Lower strata older than overlying ones (lithostratigraphy).

Faunal succession

Lower, older strata hold more primitive fossils (biostratigraphy).

Cross‑cutting relation

Intruded magma is younger than host rock – used for intrusive vulcanism.

Fragmental inclusion

Rock fragments are older than the strata containing them.

Gaps between strata = unconformities (endogenic–exogenic dynamics) → need for absolute time.

Absolute time scale

Mid‑19th century; the European geological time scale is used. Supereon → Eon (4) → Era (10) → Period → Epoch → Age.

  • Precambrian (4.6 bn – 600 Ma): Hadean; Archaean eras Eo‑, Paleo‑, Meso‑, Neo‑archaean; Proterozoic eras Paleo‑, Meso‑, Neo‑proterozoic.
  • Phanerozoic: Paleozoic (Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian), Mesozoic (Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous), Cenozoic – Paleogene (Paleocene, Eocene, Oligocene), Neogene (Miocene, Pliocene), Quaternary (Pleistocene, Holocene).
Era (notes, mnemonic APMCN)TimeLife & landforms
Azoic4600/4000–600 MaNo visible life; Precambrian; shields/cratons = basement complex, all plateaus; metalliferous minerals (degassing, swift cooling) – richest iron ore Pilbara (Hamersley) & Carajás
Paleozoic (primary)600–225 MaOld/aquatic life; Devonian = fish epoch; mass burial → coal of Carboniferous & Permian; old fold & block mountains – Appalachians (richest anthracite)
Mesozoic (secondary)225–70 MaMid‑life; quiet tectonic era (transitional drift); aquatic, terrestrial & aerial life by Cretaceous
Cenozoic (tertiary)70–1 MaNew life; active tectonics; young fold & block mountains (resource‑poor due to difficult terrain); 5 epochs
Neozoic (quaternary)1 Ma – nowPleistocene (1 Ma–10,000 yrs): youngest relief, river plains; oil & gas strata (notes). Holocene (last 10,000 yrs): Homo sapiens
Cross‑check: current ICS dates – Paleozoic from ~541 Ma, Mesozoic ~252–66 Ma, Quaternary from ~2.58 Ma.

Forces, folds & faults

Forces shaping the surface

Endogenic (tectonic / earth movements)

Diastrophism (slow): epeirogenic (continent building) & orogenic (mountain building). Sudden: earthquakes, vulcanism.

Exogenic

Gradation (erosion, transportation, deposition), weathering, mass wasting.
  • Surface relief = dynamic equilibrium of endogenic & exogenic forces; endogenic forces come from movement of lithosphere over asthenosphere.
  • Epeirogenic: vertical uplift/subsidence of large land; continents grew around shields/cratons by attachment of terranes (small shield fragments) – Chiloé Island (S America), Vancouver Island (N America).
  • Orogenic: horizontal forces → deformation/strain → folding (compression, ductile rocks) and faulting.
Folds

Anticline = up‑thrown part (peaks); syncline = down‑thrown (valleys). Geometry: limbs, hinge (where limbs meet), axial plane. Fold type depends on angle of limbs with axial plane.

TypeFeatureExample
SymmetricalLimbs at equal angle; equal stress both sidesWestern Cordillera (N America), Andes
AsymmetricalUnequal anglesHimalayan & Alpine Cordilleras
IsoclinalSymmetrical type with tilted axial planeMiddle Himalayas
RecumbentAxial plane near horizontal ("sleeping")Within all young fold ranges
Nappe (broken)Stress exceeds ductility; fold breaksSyntaxial bends of Himalaya along Indus & Tsangpo

Minor folds: fan fold (multi‑directional stress → anticlinorium/synclinorium), chevron (zig‑zag triangular), monoclinal (one limb, step‑like).

Faults
  • Fault = brittle failure after plastic deformation. Horizontal displacement = heave; vertical = throw.
  • Reverse faults (compression, brittle rock): thrust (angle ≥45°, notes) and overthrust (one slab rides horizontally over the other) – typical of the Himalayas.
  • Tensional: normal (pulling apart) and transverse (sliding past).
  • Only heave → strike‑slip fault. Heave + throw → dip‑slip fault → horst & graben / block mountain with rift valley.
  • Transform faults (minor): crust slabs moving in the same direction at different rates; strike‑slip; give lava plateaus on land and fracture zones with ancillary ridges at sea.

Continental drift, sea‑floor spreading & geomagnetism

Continental drift theory (Wegener 1915)
  • Background: Humboldt (1801) – matching shores of S Atlantic; Antonio (Snider‑)Pellegrini (1858) – first map; F.B. Taylor (1910) – drift theory.
  • Alfred Wegener (German climatologist, 1915) – from paleoclimatology: past climates didn't match present latitudes → continents drifted.
  • Mechanism: free floating of SiAl over SiMa under gravitational (equatorward) and tidal (westward) forces.
  • Pangaea ("entire earth") existed till the Carboniferous, in Panthalassa. Split into Angaraland/Laurasia (N America, Eurasia) and Gondwanaland (S America, Africa, India, Australia, Antarctica) with Tethys between.
  • Equatorward drift → buckling of Eurasia with Africa & India; Indian, Arctic & Southern Oceans formed. Westward drift → Atlantic formed, Panthalassa shrank to the Pacific (oldest ocean rocks).
Evidence for drift

Jig‑saw fit – shape

Wegener; Sir Edward Bullard (1960) computer fit at 1000 m depth. Gaps explained by coastal erosion, deposition (Niger, São Francisco deltas), eustatic and isostatic changes.

Fit of process

Tension → dip‑slip → horst‑graben: Serra da Mantiqueira & Adamawa highlands = old block mountains of a proto‑Atlantic rift valley.

Fit of structure

Matching strata and Mesosaurus fossils on both sides; gold lode deposits of Guinea coast vs placer deposits of Belém (Brazil).

Paleoclimatic ★

Gondwana lands (now tropical) share past glaciation; Angaraland had tropical forests → coal belts. Most compelling argument.
Criticism of drift theory
  • Mechanism wrong – SiAl cannot float over SiMa (Moho lies within the lithosphere); tidal force too weak.
  • Gondwana parts actually crossed the equator – equatorward/westward drift is faulty.
  • Oceanic crust treated as passive; explains only 1st‑order relief, not 2nd‑order relief.
  • Called "imaginary" and sidelined till paleomagnetism revived it.
Sea‑floor spreading (Harry Hess)
  • From study of Challenger Ridge (S Atlantic) and Albatross Plateau (SE Pacific).
  • Based on Arthur Holmes' convective magma currents in the asthenosphere – cells give diverging, converging and transverse movements.
  • New oceanic crust at spreading ridges, destruction at converging boundaries. Paired rocks on both sides of a ridge grow older with distance.
  • Oceanic crust is young (notes: not older than Paleozoic) while continental rocks go back to Azoic. Atlantic = spreading; Pacific = destructive margins.
Cross‑check: oldest surviving oceanic crust is ~180 Ma (Jurassic).
Geomagnetism & paleomagnetism
  • Magnetism from iron‑rich interior; earth like a giant bar magnet; molten core works as a dynamo. Credited to William Gilbert.
  • Declination (horizontal): angle between magnetic and true north; +ve when magnetic north is east. Gradual westward drift of the field.
  • Inclination/dip (vertical): 0° at magnetic equator, 90° at poles. Magnetic equator = aclinic line (zero dip).
  • Paleomagnetism: past field recorded in rocks – thermoremanent and depositional remanent (igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic).
  • Runcorn (1950s): apparent polar wandering – N magnetic pole from near Hawaii via E Siberia to Bathurst Island (Canada); Eurasia & N America were joined; Appalachians, Kjölen, Urals, Verkhoyansk, Chukotsky = one old orogeny → revival of drift.
  • Vine & Matthews (1963): zebra stripes – older basins reverse polarity, young ridges normal; anomalies grow with distance → proof of sea‑floor spreading.
Cross‑check: William Gilbert (De Magnete, 1600) was English, not American.

Plate tectonics & mountain building

Plate tectonic theory
  • Late 1960s; contributors: Parker, Harry Hess, Morgan, J. Tuzo Wilson and others. Lithosphere broken into plates floating over asthenosphere under convection.
  • 7 major plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo‑Australian, Antarctic (+ many minor plates).
BoundarySub‑typesFeatures
ConvergingDestructive, collidingMost important (continental crust); compression → fold orogeny, earthquakes; volcanoes only where crust is destroyed. Destructive: Circum‑Pacific & Mid‑continental belts
DivergingOceanic, continentalNew crust, ridges, rift valleys
Transverse (conservative)Transform (Tuzo Wilson)Slabs slide past: faulting, quakes. Only notable on‑land case: SW N America – California minor plate moves S/SE, rest moves N/NW → block mountains of Western Cordillera; no continuous N American trench
  • Appraisal: explains tertiary features and past boundary positions ("laws of nature don't change"). Limitation: forces only at boundaries.
  • Two later additions: Hotspots – intra‑plate divergence; Hawaiian chain from oldest Emperor Seamount to youngest forming Lōʻihi; hotspots live up to ~100 Ma. Accretion wedges – terranes attached to mainland.
Mountains & relict mountains
  • Mountains = most projected 2nd‑order relief (endogenic uplift + exogenic down‑cutting). By process: fold, block, relict.
  • Relict = worn down, no longer growing: ancient (Azoic conical remnants) and old (Paleozoic) – Caledonian (Devonian): Appalachians, Eurasian mountains, Serra da Mantiqueira; Hercynian (Permian): Great Dividing Range, Drakensberg.
  • Young (Cenozoic) mountains keep original height – fold or block.
Geosyncline theory
  • Miogeosyncline – sedimentary & metamorphic (quartzite, shale, slate). Eugeosyncline – oceanic margins, also igneous/volcanic strata.
  • Schuchert: monogeosyncline (one sedimentation + one folding; Appalachians; old fold mountains) vs polygeosyncline (many episodes, parallel ranges; Himalayas; young fold mountains).
  • Kober – 3 stages; rationalised by Arthur Holmes with convection currents:

1. Lithogenesis

Shallow broad geosyncline between stable masses (forelands) fills with sediment and becomes deep & narrow.

2. Orogenesis

Compression folds sediments into parallel ranges with a less‑disturbed medial mass – Tibetan Plateau, Hungarian plains.

3. Glyptogenesis

Complete folding with or without closing of geosyncline.

Explains only young fold mountains of the mid‑continental belt; plate tectonics explains orogenesis fully. Map: Dinaric, Carpathians, Hungarian plains; Columbia, Colorado; Kunlun, Tibet, Himalayas.

Vulcanism

Magma & ejected matter
  • All processes moving molten rock (magma) towards the surface; extrusive (volcanic) or intrusive (plutonic).
  • More silica → less mobile, more viscous; more iron → denser.
  • Basic magma – low silica (~40%), basaltic, iron‑rich, runny → plateaus. Acidic – >70% silica, granitic, viscous → cones.
  • Gases: water vapour (~70%), CO₂, SO₂. Pyroclasts = solid particles ejected.
Types of eruption (increasing intensity)
TypeNatureSetting & example
HawaiianQuietest; no trapped gas; lava streams = Pele's hairOceanic divergence; Mauna Loa
StrombolianSome gas, no rain after ejectionStromboli = "lighthouse of the Mediterranean"; continental divergence (Kilimanjaro), transverse boundary
VesuvianStrong; commonest; gases thrown high → condensation, rain → lahar (mudflow) and volcanic soilsConverging destructive boundaries: Circum‑Pacific & mid‑continental belts; Vesuvius
PeleanMost intense; cone blown off; most pyroclasts; nuée ardente (glowing clouds); no laharKrakatoa (Indonesia), Martinique (West Indies)
Volcanic cones

By composition

Basic – broad base, gentle slope, low (Mauna Loa). Acidic – narrow base, steep, tall (Vesuvius). Composite – layered strata; really every cone.

Parasitic

More than one vent/crater: Mt Shasta (USA), Fujiyama (Japan).

Cinder

Neat cone of pyroclasts: Mt Mayon (Philippines).

Caldera & plug dome

Caldera = enlarged crater (explosion/collapse); caldera lakes may have central islands – Toba (Indonesia), Taupo (NZ). Exposed pipe = plug dome (special dyke).
Intrusive features (plutons)
  • Massive plutons: batholiths (huge, deep; with xenoliths of host rock; exposed example Dartmoor, UK) and smaller stocks.
  • Tabular – concordant (parallel to bedding): sill (horizontal sheet; Palisade sill, Hudson River), laccolith (dome; Ghaap plateau, S Africa), lopolith (saucer; Bushveld, S Africa), phacolith (wave‑shaped, small).
  • Tabular – discordant (vertical): dykes; plug‑dome variety forms Ukmes range (Zimbabwe) and Sugar Loaf (Brazil).

Exogenic forces & fluvial cycle

Exogenic forces & agents
  • Levellers (Davis): weathering (in‑situ breakdown), mass wastage (gravity transfer), gradation – degradation (erosion), transportation, aggradation (deposition).
  • Agents & cycles: running water (fluvial), glacier, wind (aeolian), sea waves (coastal), underground water (karst). Running water is universal.
  • Fluvial erosion: splash (raindrops on bare sand → flash floods), sheet, gully (seasonal streamlets), stream (true river erosion).
River regimePatternExamples

CompoundLesser fluctuations; dense networkGanga, Mississippi–Missouri, Paraguay–Paraná
Davis' normal cycle of erosion
  • W.M. Davis, 1889; empirical; erosion starts after uplift ends; well‑defined stages; end‑product = featureless lowland (peneplain).
  • Landscape = f(Structure, Process, Stage). Structure: rock resistance, joints. Process: endogenic variability, exogenic levellers raising relative relief while lowering absolute height.
  • Long profile: young (source, erosion) → mature (transport) → old (mouth, deposition).

Young

Ungraded profiles; valley deepening dominant. V‑shaped valleys (differential erosion + more water), I‑shaped valleys/gorges/canyons (homogeneous rock + less water), rapids, cascades (chain of rapids), waterfalls (enlarged rapids) with plunge pools, structural benches. Shift of water divide → river piracy (elbow of capture, wind gap, misfit).

Mature

Divides eroded → graded main channels; valley widening. Flood plains, natural levees, bluffs, wide meanders with river cliff and slip‑off slope.

Old

Tributaries graded; deposition dominates. Deltas, intense meanders, ox‑bow lakes, braided channels; peneplain with monadnocks.

Appraisal of Davis
  • Dominated till plate tectonics (1960s). Penck argued uplift and erosion act together, not one after another – but died early without a full theory.
  • Plate tectonics: uplift is long diastrophism, down‑cutting can't wait; Cenozoic orogeny still on; Azoic table‑lands keep height → the word "cycle" is criticised.
  • Gilbert (American): landscape in dynamic equilibrium; used for rejuvenation studies. Davis' simplicity keeps it the main support for exogenic study.
Cross‑check: the dynamic‑equilibrium geomorphologist is G.K. Gilbert (notes write "William Gilbert").
Rejuvenation (polycyclic landforms)

Positive

Helps levelling: sea‑level rise after Pleistocene ice age (~200 m) drowning coasts; land subsidence, sea‑floor rise.

Negative

Interrupts levelling, "re‑attains youth": valley‑in‑valley (Yenisei basin, Siberia), entrenched meanders (Colorado River), knick point (break between old & new valley; can also show positive rejuvenation).

Denudation chronology, slopes & isostasy

Denudation chronology & erosional surfaces
  • Historical study of landform evolution – R.J. Small; based on Davisian cycle + Hutton's uniformitarianism. Not for tertiary relief. Depends on age of surface, rock hardness, drainage density.
  • Criticised by Bloom – too deductive; e.g. Notre Dame, Allegheny, Blue Ridge are tall due to tertiary negative rejuvenation, not hard rock.
  • Erosional surfaces cut across all structures: peneplain (fluvial), cryoplain (glacial), pediplain (aeolian), etchplain (Thomas & Pugh – savanna cycle).
  • Types: local peneplain (Mohawk Valley, Central Lowlands – Appalachians), regional (West Siberian Plain/Yenisei), re‑uplifted (mid‑course of Yenisei; valley‑in‑valley).
  • Penck's episodic erosion: Primärrumpf → waxing phase (height & relief ↑) → uniform phase → waning phase (both ↓) → Endrumpf.
Hill slopes

Every real slope is "complex compound". Young relief mostly convex, old rectilinear, ancient concave.

  • Quantitative methods – lab/cartographic gradient analysis (Wentworth and others).
  • Qualitative – mono‑process vs poly‑process. Davis – slope decline: steep convex (young) → declining, rectilinear (mature) → concave with remnant convex parts (old); most followed school.
  • Penck – slope replacement: homogeneous cliff with a non‑eroding, non‑depositing river at foot; free face is replaced by concave slope. Fits sea cliffs (→ wave‑cut platform) and glacial horn collapse.
Isostasy
  • Equilibrium between crustal features despite height/depth differences. Started with Sir George Everest's Himalayan survey – ground vs astronomical distances differed due to plumb deflection.
  • Term isostasy given by Dutton – "floating crust in gravitational balance".

Pratt

"Different density, same depth": taller features are lighter; compensated at a level of compensation. Explains CC (lighter granite) vs OC (denser basalt). Followed by Hayford & Bowie.

Airy

"Same density, different depth": taller features have deeper roots. Explains intra‑crustal features. Supported by Heiskanen.

Both are seen as complementary.

  • Endogenic adjustment: uplift loads crust → sinks, grows roots, displaces magma.
  • Exogenic adjustment: erosion unloads crust → rebound (ancient relief).
  • Climatic: ice‑age load sinks continents; after melting, continents rebound while loaded ocean crust sinks – Scandinavian coast getting shallower, hurting ports.
