Geopolitics & Mackinder's Heartland

Two streams of geopolitics

Organic state

Ratzel (state as organism that grows or dies) → Kjellén (not in syllabus) → Haushofer (German).

Geopolitical ideas

Sea power: Mahan → Mackinder → Spykman. Air power: Meinig(?) / de Seversky line → Cohen (notes list "Mezhen" & Cohen).

Ratzel's organic approach dominated early 20th century but declined. American Mahan stressed that political importance comes from location (sea power). British Mackinder enlarged these ideas into dynamic, applicable ones. In syllabus: Mackinder – Heartland, Spykman – Rimland.

Cross‑check: notes call Mackinder Mahan's "disciple" – he built on sea‑power debates but was not his student.
1904 – "The Geographical Pivot of History"
  • Presented to the Royal Geographical Society. Natural fortress = area sea power can't reach.
  • Pivot area – from the Volga to Siberia, with frozen river mouths and the unnavigable Arctic Ocean; potential node of world power.
  • Inner / marginal crescent – Manchuria to Scandinavia incl. N Africa, SW, S & SE Asia.
  • Outer / insular crescent – New World, southern Africa, British & Japanese islands.
  • World Island = Eurasia + Africa (largest land & population). First map of the world in geopolitical terms.
1919 – "Democratic Ideals and Reality"
  • Shaped by W Europe's economic rise, German invasion of Tsarist Russia and the 1917 October Revolution.
  • Heartland = enlarged pivot: USSR + Danube valley, Poland, Tibet, China, Asia Minor (Turkey), Persia (Iran). Inner crescent shrank; outer crescent same.
  • Heartland can be entered only via the European steppes, hence: "Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland; who rules the Heartland commands the World Island; who rules the World Island commands the World."
  • Projection matched the USSR's later rise as a world power – most practical geopolitical idea.
1943 – "The Round World and the Winning of the Peace"
  • Kept the heartland but added Midland (North Atlantic capitalist cooperation to balance the heartland).
  • Recognised "adversity zones" as Lenaland – notes: from the Lena via East Siberian mountains to Bering Strait, Alaska and Western Cordillera.

Appraisal of Heartland & Spykman's Rimland

Appraisal of Heartland
  • Dynamic and empirical; anticipated the Cold War; valid till USSR's end (1991). Criticised from the 1940s:
  • Mercator projection – true shape but area distortion, making high‑latitude heartland look huge.
  • Ignored air power despite WWII – Sprout (1965): he kept the "natural protected fort" idea.
  • Ignored adversities inside the heartland – strongest critic Spykman ("area of adversity").
Rimland concept (1944)
  • Heartland = zone of adversities: only partial centrality, harsh relief & climate, little arable land, few minerals/energy, cut off from sea trade routes, sparse and primitive population → its potential "imaginary".
  • Rimland: maritime Europe, oil‑rich West Asia, agricultural/mineral/population nodes of South & East Asia, maritime SE Asia – nearly all of Eurasia around the heartland.
  • "Who controls the Rimland rules Eurasia; who rules Eurasia controls the destinies of the world."
  • Little support in the 1940s (USSR was real); after 1991 adversities of Central Asia (except Kazakhstan), Belarus, Moldova, Siberia became clear. Post‑Cold War, Eurasian rimlands – China, India, Russia, Gulf – matter for US strategy; rise of China & India shifts the economic node east.
  • Much referred to, but less practically "proven" than the heartland was.
Cross‑check: Spykman's 1944 book is "The Geography of the Peace" (notes: "Geography of Place").

International boundaries & frontiers

Nation, state, frontier, boundary
  • Nation = feeling of belonging; state = concrete political territory.
  • Frontiers (buffer zones, no‑man's land, transition zones) gave way to boundaries = lines bounding a state's territory.
  • Types – spatial: geographic (mountain, river, lake), cultural, geometric; temporal: antecedent, consequent, relict (superimposed).
Spatial types
TypeNatureExamples
MountainMostly antecedent (before cultures formed); blocks exchange, no frontier growthHimalayas (India–China), Andes (Chile–Argentina), Pyrenees (Spain–France)

LakeConsequent; stability depends on relationsGreat Lakes (except Michigan) – most stable (USA–Canada); L. Victoria (Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania) & L. Chad (Niger, Chad, Nigeria) – disputed

Relict boundaries no longer exist politically; notes link them to superimposed boundaries drawn without people's choice.

Delimitation & demarcation
  • Delimitation – talks on geographic feasibility and stability, ending in a treaty/agreement.
  • Demarcation – actual marking on the ground. In Africa, allocation and delimitation were skipped. Even demarcated boundaries can change later.

Indian states reorganisation

Three phases: consolidation → linguistic → economic.

Phase 1: Consolidation
  • At Independence >40% of territory was under princely states (notes). Department of States integrated them, largely through public opinion.
  • Trouble spots: Junagadh, Hyderabad (joined after delay/negotiation) and J&K – part held by Pakistan, LoC after UN Security Council intervention; notes call it a failure of the consolidation phase.
Phase 2: Linguistic (1956 onward)
  • Language had political recognition from the freedom struggle ("non‑divisive" regional consciousness). Telugu agitation → States Reorganisation Commission → 1956 Act.
  • 1956: Telugu AP (Telangana from Hyderabad merged), Tamil TN; Kerala = Malabar + Travancore–Cochin; Kannada areas of Bombay, Madras, Hyderabad & Coorg → Mysore; Bombay enlarged with Kutch, Saurashtra, Marathi parts of Hyderabad.
  • 1960: Bombay → Gujarat & Maharashtra.
  • 1966: Hindi Haryana from Punjabi Punjab (last linguistic split); Pahari Kangra to HP (then UT); Chandigarh UT and joint capital.
Phase 3: Economic (1970s onward)
  • 1970s: HP, Meghalaya, Manipur, Tripura raised to statehood; 1980s: Mizoram, Arunachal, Goa.
  • Sikkim joined in 1975 after a referendum (former protectorate).
  • 2000: Uttarakhand, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand. Telangana later; demands remain – Vidarbha, Bodoland, Gorkhaland.
Cross‑check: notes also list Assam among 1970s "elevations" – Assam was already a state; Telangana was formed in 2014.

India and its neighbours

India's geopolitical sensitivity: tussled relations with neighbours, failure of SAARC, Indian Ocean, India–China regional manifestations. Land frontier >15,000 km.

India–Pakistan
  • ~3300 km; demarcated in Punjab, Rajasthan, Gujarat and parts of J&K. Disputes: PoK and Sir Creek.
  • LoC ~740 km; northernmost point Indira Col; beyond lies Siachen with the Actual Ground Position Line.
  • Shaksgam valley (~5000 km²) ceded by Pakistan to China in 1963.
  • Sir Creek – the 1968 Kutch tribunal settlement isn't accepted by Pakistan; strategic and oil‑gas rich.
India–China
  • ~3500 km (notes); LAC is not a final boundary. Disputes in J&K, Uttarakhand, Arunachal.
  • J&K: Aksai Chin (~35,000 km² per notes) + Shaksgam; Uttarakhand: Barahoti plains; Arunachal: China rejects the McMahon Line. China also questions Sikkim's accession.
Cross‑check: Aksai Chin is usually given as ~38,000 km².
India–Bangladesh & India–Nepal
  • Bangladesh – longest border (~4100 km), porous; illegal migration; New Moore Island, Char lands (~300 km² of Padma alluvium); enclave swap to fix partition anomalies.
  • Nepal – ~1700 km; river‑water issues – Kosi, Kali (Sharda).

SAARC

Background & phases
  • South Asia: poverty, ethnic & sectarian conflict, terrorism, Maoism, nuclear risk; strongly Indocentric. Talks from early 1980s; launched 1985.
PhaseFeatures
1 (1985–1995)Genesis; institutions; cooperation in non‑controversial areas; India–Pakistan distrust
2 (1995–2005)Formal programming after Cold War; SIPA (SAARC Integrated Programme of Action)
3 (2005–)Afghanistan as member; observers USA, S Korea, Japan, China, EU; regional agreements
Cross‑check: Afghanistan became a member in 2007 (admission agreed 2005).
Achievements & failure
  • SIPA – audio‑visual exchange, chambers of commerce, parliamentarians, documentation, law forums.
  • Regional agreements – food security, suppression of terrorism, child welfare, women & drug trafficking, greener South Asia; SAPTA → SAFTA.
  • Failure causes – bureaucratic hurdles (primary), sub‑regional arrangements, weak infrastructure, lack of leadership; globalism over regionalism; disparities, non‑complementarity, tariffs; India's Look East vs Pakistan's westward policies; India reluctant to lead.

Geopolitics of the Indian Ocean

From British Lake to Zone of Peace
  • 3rd largest; continent/half ocean. For >150 years a "British Lake": links Karachi–Yangon, Aden–Singapore, Port Elizabeth–Perth; HQ Colombo.
  • British retreat by the 1960s left a vacuum; fleets and submarines of big powers moved in. On rim countries' request the UN declared it a Zone of Peace. UNCLOS zoning applies except BIOT.
Why the IOR matters

Resources

Persian Gulf oil (Saudi Arabia); >50% of crude trade by sea (notes, WTO); BoB gas; tidal energy in north; fisheries; Mn nodules; salt.

Trade routes

Transit between Pacific & Atlantic; chokepoints – Suez, Malacca, Hormuz, Bab‑el‑Mandeb, Lombok; Cape of Good Hope; port investment (China most aggressive).

Strategic position

Near rimlands; nuclear/ICBM era; US base at Diego Garcia (Chagos, BIOT); Chinese anti‑piracy fleets.

Sensitive South Asia

Conflicts and nuclear neighbours.
India–China regional manifestations
  • Both seek resource security outside their borders (esp. Africa) – rising mutual sensitivity.
  • India: "Diamond string" connectivity – Trans‑Asian Railway links, Asian Highway (Dhaka–Kolkata–Delhi), Sittwe port (Myanmar) & Kaladan river via Mizoram; anti‑piracy patrols since 2008 (490 nm transit corridor, Gulf of Aden); Look East Policy (1991) – ASEAN Regional Forum, East Asia Summit, BIMSTEC, Mekong–Ganga Cooperation; A&N tri‑service command.
  • China: String of Pearls – sea lanes from Hong Kong to Port Sudan via Malacca, Hormuz, Bab‑el‑Mandeb; SHADE (Shared Awareness and Deconfliction) in Gulf of Aden; land side – mining in Afghanistan, links to Central Asian gas. India counters with TAPI pipeline and strategic partnership with Afghanistan.

Geographical basis of federation, regionalism & integration

  • Basis of federation – diversity: physical (size, relief, climate, geology, soil), demographic (size, stage, age‑sex, ethnicity, language, religion), economic (resources, agro‑climate, industry, transport).
  • Regional consciousness – "place‑belongingness"; causes: physical, demographic, economic, and multi‑party democracy with regional parties. Frontiers can be divisive or non‑divisive; in India mostly non‑divisive but cause unrest.
  • National integration – unitary physiography, unitary climate (monsoon), unifying economy & transport, shared Indic cultural identity, unitary constitutional provisions.

Indian security concerns

Terrorism
  • 1970s terrorism – regional, clear aims, selective violence. Modern – complex, ideological/religious, transnational, unpredictable.
  • Factors: communication technology; diffuse layered groups & sleeper cells; state sponsorship; fundamentalism; bad governance and deprivation.
  • India: cross‑border terrorism; in J&K early groups gave way to more radical ones (e.g. LeT); Afghan jihad of the 1980s linked South Asian terror globally.
  • Agencies: RAW (external), IB (MHA, internal), joint intelligence committee, DIA; NSG, SPG; CRPF, CISF, BSF. Law & order is a state subject – police respond first. Proposed NCTC (US model) opposed by states. NIA created after the 2008 Mumbai attack – works across states without special permission.
Cross‑check: NIA = National Investigation Agency (notes: "Information").
Left‑wing extremism
  • Stages: Naxal movement → Maoist movement → LWE.
  • Naxalbari (Darjeeling), late 1960s – farmers vs landlords; 1969 formal party; spread in WB & Telangana. 2004 merger formed CPI (Maoist); banned under UAPA in 2009; extortion from mining.
  • Red Corridor – notes: >170 districts in 16 states, from Nepal–Bihar border to Kerala–Karnataka border.
  • Causes: lack of development (no roads, water, schools, hospitals, police – no visible administration); legal concerns ignored (Fifth Schedule tribal councils, land ceiling under Ninth Schedule implemented only in J&K, WB, Kerala; mining by MNCs); ad‑hoc counter‑policies.
Cross‑check: 1969 party was CPI (Marxist‑Leninist); CPI (Maoist) formed from PWG + MCC in 2004. LWE districts are now far fewer than in 2014.
